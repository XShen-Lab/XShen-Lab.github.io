XShen Lab · Research

Biological Information Flow

From Genome to Cell Fate

How does sparse and stochastic genome activity become stable biological information and cell fate?

We study the nucleus as a dynamic information-processing system in which genome organization, RNA networks and transcriptional surveillance transform molecular fluctuation into stable cellular identity.

Organization → Processing → Selection → Stabilization

Scientific vision

Dynamic molecular activity. Ordered biological states.

Although cell types share essentially the same genome, they acquire diverse and persistent identities while transcription, RNA metabolism and chromatin organization fluctuate across space and time.

Biological information therefore cannot be understood as a simple DNA → RNA → protein pathway. It is continuously organized, interpreted and selected through the noncoding genome, nuclear architecture, RNA regulatory networks and transcriptional environments.

Our long-term goal is a predictive framework, grounded in physical principles and statistical theories, for understanding cellular states in development, aging and disease.

Four connected research programs

One information system, viewed in sequence.

Move horizontally from the organization of the genome to the stabilization of cell fate. Each program asks a distinct question, but the mechanism flows continuously across the four stages.

01 Organization

Organizing Biological Information

Genome Organization

How does genome sequence encode nuclear organization?

Genome sequence encodes more than genes. We investigate how repetitive elements—including L1 and B1/Alu sequences—establish principles of chromosome compartmentalization and nuclear positioning, and how their RNA products participate in chromatin organization.

We are extending this framework to understand genome partitioning and remodeling during development, cell-cycle transitions and changes in cell fate.

Current focus

  • Repetitive elements and their RNA products
  • Chromosome compartmentalization and nuclear positioning
  • Dynamic genome remodeling across cellular transitions
02 Processing

Processing Biological Information

RNA Networks

How does RNA organize the nuclear environment?

RNA is both a carrier of genetic information and a material organizer of nuclear function. Noncoding RNAs regulate neighboring genes and local chromatin states, while RNA-binding proteins form dynamic RNA-associated condensates that influence RNA Polymerase II recruitment, CTD phosphorylation and elongation.

We investigate how nascent RNA, RNA-binding proteins and processing factors assemble an RNA–RBP meshwork that coordinates transcription, RNA maturation, RNA degradation and nuclear organization.

Current focus

  • Nascent RNA and RNA-binding proteins
  • RNA-associated condensates and Pol II regulation
  • Coupling of transcription, processing and nuclear architecture
03 Selection

Selecting Biological Information

Transcriptional Surveillance

How does productive information emerge from pervasive transcription?

Eukaryotic genomes are extensively transcribed, yet only a fraction of transcriptional events achieve productive elongation and generate mature mRNAs. We therefore treat productive transcription as an actively established state rather than a default outcome.

Our work examines how the RNA Polymerase II CTD, EIF4A3, RNA processing and degradation pathways form a genome-scale surveillance system that clears nonproductive transcription while supporting RNA maturation, productive elongation and polymerase recycling.

Current focus

  • RNA Polymerase II CTD and EIF4A3
  • Kinetic selection of productive elongation
  • RNA maturation, clearance and polymerase recycling

04 Stabilization

Stabilizing Biological Information

Cell Fate Dynamics

How do probabilistic genome activities generate stable cell identity?

Stable identity need not require every cell to execute an identical transcriptional program. With scFLUENT-seq, developed in our laboratory, we obtain a short-pulse snapshot of nascent genome usage at single-cell resolution.

Individual cells use a cell-type-dependent fraction of approximately 0.02%–3.1% of the genome at a given moment, while populations explore broader genomic landscapes. We model cellular identity as a stable probability distribution emerging from coordinated stochastic activity.

Current focus

Transcriptional Surveillance · Mechanism in focus

Working models and ongoing research

Kinetic selection turns pervasive transcription into productive output.

We distinguish a widespread basal state, in which elongation complexes frequently drop off, from a processive state that supports proper nascent RNP assembly and productive elongation. Transcriptional surveillance links RNA Polymerase II CTD state, EIF4A3 and RNA-processing pathways to this kinetic transition.

Three views of the same transition

CTD → RNP → Pol II fate

The nucleus as a system

Two interwoven polymer networks shape information flow.

The nucleus is a far-from-equilibrium polymer–electrolyte system. Chromatin carries genetic information and provides the structural basis for genome folding; the RNA–RBP meshwork, composed of nascent RNA, RNA-binding proteins and RNA-processing factors, helps determine when, where and how genomic information is accessed.

Their interface—the Perichromatin Region—is a dynamic surface where transcription, RNA processing, RNP assembly, RNA degradation and nuclear transport are spatially and functionally coupled. Transcription continually introduces RNA, energy and entropy into this active system.

Future vision

Toward a predictive theory of cell state.

We aim to move beyond gene-centric and pathway-centric descriptions toward physical principles, statistical theories and predictive models that connect DNA sequence, nuclear organization, RNA processing, transcriptional selection and cell fate.
