To me, science is a serious game played by children.

Life is like an elephant whose whole form we cannot see. We can often touch only a single molecule, a pathway, or a phenotype, but I want to know: How are these local pieces connected? How does life transform random, inefficient, and even chaotic molecular activity into stable biological information and cell fate?

This is precisely the question our lab is trying to answer.

Using “Biological Information Flow” as our framework, we study how genome sequence, chromatin structure, RNA networks, and transcriptional dynamics work together to organize, select, and stabilize biological information. We ask how repetitive sequences organize the three-dimensional genome, how nascent RNA builds nuclear RNP networks, how transcriptional surveillance selects productive transcription from pervasive and failure-prone genome activity, and how stochastic and sparse genome usage in single cells emerges as stable cell states.

We believe that the complexity of life does not necessarily mean that its fundamental rules must also be complex. We hope to move beyond ever-growing collections of molecules and arrows, to see the whole through the details and distill principles from phenomena: let imagination guide experiments and analysis, let experiments test imagination, let theory penetrate phenomena, and let computation connect different scales.

If you come from physics, mathematics, computer science, engineering, chemistry, or another science or engineering background—and are genuinely curious about life—I especially welcome you to rotate in the lab. You do not need to be familiar with molecular biology or already know how to conduct experiments. What matters more is that you are willing to enter an unfamiliar field and bring your quantitative thinking, modeling skills, or engineering intuition into the life sciences.

A project here might begin with a microscopy image, an unexpected data distribution, or a seemingly naïve question. You might need to learn single-cell omics and quantitative imaging; you might also need to build physical models, write code, use AI, or even reconstitute a life process with your own hands.

I will be deeply involved in your training—posing questions, challenging assumptions, and pushing you beyond familiar boundaries. Early in your scientific training, you need to be willing to trust and accept my guidance. This does not mean obedience. It means gradually developing independent questions, scientific judgment, and creativity through sustained discussion, questioning, and training.

I value students who are ambitious without being arrogant, imaginative while respecting evidence, willing to take risks while also mastering the fundamentals. It is fine not to understand something yet, but you must be willing to learn. It is fine to ask an immature question, but do not be afraid of looking foolish.

A rotation is also a chance for us to get to know one another. If you are only looking for a safe, familiar, step-by-step project, this lab may not be the right fit. But if you are willing to follow the faint light within you, enter the life sciences, embrace training, and ultimately make your own path—come talk to me.

Let us not remain in one place, repeatedly touching the same part of the elephant. Let us go farther and reach more widely.

Welcome to this serious game played by children.<br>
Welcome to becoming a wanderer of the infinite.
